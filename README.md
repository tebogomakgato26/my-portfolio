# Agile Project Portfolio: Community Security Business Soshanguve

**Project:** Community Security Business Soshanguve, Gauteng

My team's project is a community-based security business for Soshanguve. I joined the team in the second semester. The team also planned its work on a Trello board called **SAFEGUARD**, which holds the Product Backlog and the sprint lists shown in this portfolio.

### Project Objective

The objective of this business is to provide affordable, reliable, and community-based security services to residents of Soshanguve, improving safety through rapid emergency response, local employment, and the use of a mobile alert system.

"A safer Soshanguve - where every resident, scholar, and small business owner can live and work without fear, supported by an affordable, locally operated security service built by and for the community."

An Agile Team Charter is an agreement that a team makes at the start of a project. It explains why the team exists, who does what, how the team talks to each other and what the team wants to achieve. The charter below is my team's real charter for this project.

Our team charter is the agreement that explains how the team works together. It was drawn up when the project started, and I joined the team in the second semester. The charter names five team members and gives each person a clear role. My role is Presenter, which means I present the team's work to lecturers and explain our progress clearly. In future projects I would look at the charter with the team at the end of every sprint and check whether we followed our own rules. I would also write down my role's tasks more clearly so the team can see what the Presenter prepares before each presentation.

## 2. User Stories and Tasks

### 2.1 What are User Stories and Tasks?

A **user story** is a short sentence that says what a person wants from the application and why. It is written like this: "As a [type of user], I want [something], so that [reason]."

A **task** is a small piece of development work that must be done to build that feature. On our Trello board every card has a checklist, and each item on the checklist is a task.

Our SAFEGUARD board has two kinds of cards. Some are user stories, for example "Edit or remove emergency contacts at any time". Others are technical set-up work, for example "Set up JWT authentication and role-based authorization", "Integrate FCM for push notifications" and "Set up React project structure" in Sprint 1.

### 2.2 My User Stories

These are the user stories that I worked on. Each one is written in full in the description of its Trello card, and the picture under each story is the card itself.

### 2.5 Personal Reflection

On our board, the user story "Edit or remove emergency contacts at any time" in Sprint 2 and the user story "Log shift end by checking out on the app" in Sprint 3 are assigned to me. Each story has a checklist of four tasks: design the screen, build the feature, save the data where needed, and test it. The checklists show 4/4 completed for both stories.

This showed me the difference between a story and a task. The story tells us what the resident or the guard needs. The tasks tell the developer what to do step by step. I also learned that testing must be its own task, because a feature is not finished when it is only built.

Writing stories in the "As a ..., I want ..., so that ..." format makes me think about the person using the app before I think about the code. In future projects I would write the "so that" part more carefully for every card, and I would keep the checklist up to date as I finish each task so that the team can always see the real progress.

A sprint is a short, fixed period of work. A Sprint Backlog is the part of the Product Backlog that the team chooses to finish in one sprint. The Product Backlog is the big list of everything. The Sprint Backlog is the small list for now.

On our Trello board there are **16 sprint lists**, from Sprint 1 to Sprint 16. Most sprints hold 5 cards (Sprint 1 holds 6 and Sprint 15 holds 2). Every card has a due date, and many have a checklist, so the team can see how much of the work is done. A last list called **Sprint | Completed** holds 82 cards.

In future projects I would write a one-line goal for each sprint, hold a short review at the end of it, and tick off my checklist items on the same day that I finish each task.

## 5. Agile Logbook

### 5.1 What is an Agile Logbook?

An Agile Logbook is the diary of the project. It records what each team member did, what they plan to do next and what is blocking them. My logbook uses the daily stand-up format, with three questions every day:

1. What did I complete since the last stand-up?
2. What am I planning to complete between now and the next stand-up?
3. What are my impediments (risks or problems)?

It also has a **Taskboard**, which shows what is in the Product Backlog, In Progress and Completed, and a **Walkthrough**, which is a short team check of the board.

### 5.2 What my logbook shows

My logbook has three parts: a **Taskboard** (what is in the Product Backlog, In Progress and Completed), a **Walkthrough** (a short team check of the board) and **daily stand-up entries** for Monday 4 August to Thursday 7 August 2026. In these four days I reviewed our presentation, updated the Product Backlog and the Trello board with the team, presented the backlog to the lecturer, received feedback, corrected it and presented the revised version. ### 5.4 Personal Reflection

My Agile Logbook records four days of work from 4 to 7 August 2026. In that week my main activity was the Product Backlog: I reviewed our presentation and updated the backlog with the team, presented it to the lecturer, received feedback, corrected it and presented the revised version. The logbook also shows my problems honestly. On 4 August some backlog items needed to be reorganised, and on 6 August the backlog needed corrections before it could be approved. On 5 and 7 August I wrote that I had no major problems.

Writing the three stand-up questions every day made me think about what I had actually finished, what came next and what was in my way. The Walkthrough also helped, because it checked that nobody was working on something that was not on the Trello board. This links to our charter value of Transparency, which asks us to be honest about progress and challenges.

I learned that a logbook is more than a diary. It is evidence of how the project moved forward day by day, and it helped me see how feedback led to improvements. In future projects I would add a short retrospective at the end of each sprint that records what went well, what did not and what we will change. I would also keep the entries going every working day so that no part of the project history is missing.
